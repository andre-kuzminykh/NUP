AI has outrun us

2026: capability shot ahead, while people, processes and laws lag behind

AI Index · 2025 → 2026
2025 → 2026
Capability is growing faster than everything around it can adjust

I finally sat down and read the fresh Stanford AI Index cover to cover — all 400-plus pages — and compared it with last year's edition. What's interesting isn't the 2026 snapshot itself, but the dynamic: what moved, and in which direction, over the year. And the thing that jumps out most — AI itself shot ahead, while everything around it: people, companies, laws, education, the job market — is failing to keep up.

What follows is a comparison of the two Stanford HAI AI Index editions, 2025 and 2026 — an independent study that has spent nearly a decade gathering data across the field. I looked not at individual records but at what changed year over year: where things are heading, and how fast. First the facts and the trajectory, then what follows from them.

Money: a doubling in a year

The first thing that jumps out is money. U.S. private AI investment grew from $109B to $286B in a year. Global corporate investment more than doubled, generative AI added over 200% and took nearly half of all private money. Newly funded AI companies rose 71%, and billion-dollar rounds nearly doubled.

And this is no longer cautious interest — it's the whole industry betting ten years ahead. With one big caveat: revenue is growing fast, but spending on compute is growing faster. Google alone poured more than $150B into infrastructure in a year. Whether those buildouts pay off, no one yet knows — but it's too late to back out.

Adoption: from toy to infrastructure

Money follows demand — and demand went mass-market. The share of companies using AI is now 88%, up from 78% a year ago and 55% two years before. At 70%, it's built into at least one workflow. And among ordinary people, generative AI reached 53% in three years — faster than the computer or the internet did.

But here's the first gap between the noise and reality. "We use AI" — 88%. But full AI agents, the kind that carry a task to completion on their own, have been deployed by single-digit percentages of companies. Between "we switched on ChatGPT" and "we have an agent running in production" lies a chasm, and most are still standing at its edge.

And one more number. The value people get from free AI tools grew 54% in a year: from $112B to $172B in the U.S., and per user it tripled. Almost all of it is free. Enormous value is created and handed out for nothing; what that leads to will become clear a little later.

Capability: brilliant and helpless at once

On raw capability there's no ambiguity: AI got noticeably smarter, fast. On the main coding test, models climbed from 60% to nearly 100% of the human level in a year. On an exam built to be hard for machines — plus 30 points in a year. AI won gold at the Math Olympiad and now finishes two-thirds of real computer tasks — up from 12% to 66%.

And the very same model that wins olympiad gold can't read an analog clock — it gets the time right only half the time, while humans get 90%. Robots handle just 12% of household chores. Researchers call this the jagged frontier: AI is powerful and helpless within a single step, and no average score tells you where it will trip. Science shows the same gap — AI beats human chemists, yet reproduces an already-published study less than 20% of the time.

So the lesson for anyone putting AI into a real product is simple and hard: don't trust the average score or the pretty rankings. Test it on your own task — especially where a mistake is expensive.

The best models are now nearly identical

A year ago the top models were visibly different. Now, barely. Six leading labs run neck and neck, and the gap between the best U.S. and Chinese model is just 2.7% (a year ago China's DeepSeek-R1 even caught up with the U.S. leaders for a while). Open models you can download for free trail by 3.3%.

And that flips the economics. When a strong model is available to everyone and keeps getting cheaper, the model itself stops being an advantage. The fight moves to cost, reliability and narrow specialization. And cheaper doesn't mean worse: small models increasingly beat giants hundreds of times their size. When DeepSeek showed a strong result for a fraction of the money in early 2025, U.S. tech lost more than a trillion dollars of value in a single day — the market suddenly grasped that cheap, open AI breaks the familiar business models.

Our ruler is breaking too. The tests everyone relies on "run out of road" within months, and up to 42% of the questions in popular ones turn out to be broken. We're losing our measuring instruments exactly when we need them most.

Risk is not keeping pace with capability

While AI grows exponentially, safety limps behind. Documented AI failures and incidents rose to 362, up from 233 a year ago. Almost every developer reports capability results; on safety, reporting is spotty and reluctant. Medicine shows it live: in 2025 the U.S. regulator cleared 258 AI devices for clinics — most without new clinical trials, and only 2.4% backed by randomized-trial data. The technology is rolling into hospitals faster than the evidence that it can be trusted.

Then the physical footprint. Training a single model, Grok 4, is estimated at 72,816 tons of CO₂. AI data-center power reached 29.6 GW — like a whole New York state at peak demand. And almost every frontier chip is made by one Taiwanese foundry — so the entire global AI stack rests on a single point that could one day fail.

Work: the quiet change starts at the bottom

The most underrated part of the 2026 data is the job market. The gains from AI are already measurable: +14–15% in support, +26% in development, up to +50% in marketing. But the effect is uneven, and not everyone feels it first.

Here's the key detail. Employment of developers aged 22–25 fell nearly 20% since 2024 — while more experienced engineers are being hired. AI didn't "replace programmers." It took the routine — the very work juniors used to grow on. The first rung of the career ladder is simply vanishing. Students already feel it: enrollment in CS majors dropped 11% in a year. And a third of companies admit they plan to cut people in the coming year.

Geopolitics: everyone pulls their own way

Governments got serious about AI — but each in its own direction. Europe's first AI Act bans took effect (on emotion recognition and "predictive policing," for example); the U.S., by contrast, lifted rules in the name of "leadership"; Japan, South Korea and Italy passed their own laws. More than half of new national strategies came from developing countries. And at the Paris summit the U.S. and Britain flat-out refused to sign a joint declaration that 60 countries endorsed — the divergence is plain.

The strongest models are still made in the U.S. and China, but open source is reshuffling the players: the "rest of the world" on GitHub already outpaces Europe and is closing on the U.S. Openness became the great equalizer — and, at the same time, a way to not depend on someone else's technology.

People: calmer and more anxious at once

The public is reading mixed signals. The share who see more benefit than harm in AI rose to 59% from 55% a year ago — yet the share who say AI makes them nervous is now 52%. The most telling divide, though, is between experts and ordinary people.

Usually that means one thing: the public hasn't yet seen what the people working with AI every day already see.

What to do with all this

The model is no longer an advantage. When six labs run neck and neck and free models trail by a few percent, strong AI is available to everyone. So it's not about the model anymore. The winner is whoever rebuilt everything around it: how tasks are framed, where the data lives, how results get checked, who's accountable. Just handing your team access to a neural network doesn't speed up work — it speeds up the mess.

Hence the new core skill. Not "writing code," but owning the outcome: framing the task, gathering context, checking it, carrying it to a product. A weak specialist with AI just does bad work faster. A strong one starts running a whole system. And yes, juniors felt it first: their work is exactly what AI took first.

Building became nearly free. $172B of value people get for free is a mountain of value almost no one has learned to turn into money yet. The barrier to entry collapsed: where you once needed a team costing a million a year, one person with agents is now enough. So the winner is not whoever has the better model but whoever can get a product to people and keep them. Investor money has stopped being a precondition for building an AI company.

And one more thing. Since we're running faster than we can measure, judging quality becomes your job, not the leaderboard's. For anyone putting AI into a real product — especially in fintech and other strict fields — that means tests, acceptance criteria, traceability, dashboards. Without them, working with agents turns into expensive vibe-coding.

And the main thing. The question is no longer whether AI works — it works. The question is whether you're ready to use it. The technology has already outrun us. What's left is to see who catches up faster — not in capability, but in how it's built inside: people, processes, ownership.

The essence of 2026
AI grew faster than everything around it.
And now it's not about the model, but about people, processes and ownership.
